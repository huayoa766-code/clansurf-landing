The problem

Eggs from two doors down shouldn’t need a server in another country.

More and more of what people need every day, from food and water to repairs, now reaches them through an app. The app runs on someone else’s servers, takes a cut, answers to its investors, and stops working when the connection drops.

Meanwhile the neighbour who sells eggs stays invisible to the people fifty metres away. They start to think nobody wants what they make.

How it works

Everything runs offline. It syncs when it can.

The framework has three layers. The first two never need the internet. The third keeps an honest record and publishes it whenever a connection turns up, so anyone can check what happened.

  1. Offline

    Hardware

    Inexpensive LoRa radios carry messages across the neighbourhood, each one repeating for the next. People can discover and reach each other with no mobile data at all.

    The locality buys the kit directly and owns it outright. We don’t sell hardware.

  2. Offline

    Community

    The node itself: up to ten providers, the households around them and one node manager. The people who live there decide who joins, who leaves and who leads.

    Clansurf never decides who belongs.

  3. Syncs

    Governance node

    Every delivery and receipt is signed on the device and added to a local record that can’t be quietly changed. When a connection is available, the record is anchored on a public chain, so it can be checked from anywhere.

    It records what was delivered, not money. Clansurf never handles payments.

If the connection is gone for a week, nothing stops. The record is just a week late.

Built to last

No cloud, no gatekeeper, no cut.

A platform is only as reliable as its servers and its funding. A node belongs to the street it serves, so it keeps working when either one fails.

Questions

The things people ask first.

There are already apps for finding local sellers.

Those sellers existed long before the apps. The apps didn’t create the carpenter or the milkman. They put a uniform on them and built a gate in front. A node makes them visible and leaves them independent.

So is this a hardware project?

No. The radios are a tool. What really goes into a neighbourhood is governance: who belongs, who maintains the node, what’s public and who decides. The hardware lets all of that run without anyone’s permission.

Is it a payments or crypto thing?

No. The node records that something was delivered and who produced it. It never moves money. The public chain is used only so that anyone can check the record hasn’t been changed.

What does Clansurf get paid for?

Choosing the kit, setting up the node, designing the rules it runs on, and training the node manager until they don’t need us. Then we leave, and everything belongs to the locality. We never take a share of trade, sell anyone’s data, or claim a neighbourhood as ours.

Where can I read the details?

The working paper covers the radio bands by country, the governance rules, how the record works offline and what happens in a blackout. It’s at paper.clansurf.com.
